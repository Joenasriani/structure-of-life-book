P. 04
WHY THIS BOOK EXISTS

Life usually presents the inverse problem.

We see behavior but not the whole state. We observe effects but not every cause. We inherit bodies, minds, families, institutions and environments whose rules were not handed to us. We see partial histories and noisy measurements. We act inside the system while trying to understand it. Often the system changes while we are studying it.

Which structures genuinely help us infer how a complex system works, which only transfer as analogy, and what evidence would tell us that our model is wrong?

P. 10
CHAPTER 01 / THE MODEL IS NOT THE WORLD

A system becomes thinkable by being reduced.

A representation is any selected description of a target in terms of variables, relations, states, symbols or categories. An abstraction deliberately suppresses detail. A model goes one step further: it uses a structured representation to explain, predict, simulate or intervene.

Expertise research shows why this matters: the representation changes which solutions become visible.

A useful model is the smallest representation that preserves the structure needed for the current question.

P. 13
CHAPTER 02 / FRAME BEFORE YOU SOLVE

A problem is not merely found. It is formulated.

A problem frame specifies the target being explained or changed, the variables treated as relevant, the evidence that will count, the stakeholders whose outcomes matter, and the criterion by which an intervention will be judged.

The first reverse-engineering discipline is to write the frame before optimizing inside it.

P. 16
CHAPTER 03 / WHAT CAN BE SEEN

The most important state may be hidden.

Reverse engineering begins with a limitation: we observe outputs, partial inputs, traces, delays and consequences. We usually do not observe every internal variable that produced them. That makes observability and identifiability fundamental.

Several internal systems can generate similar outputs. The correct result may be a narrower claim, not a more confident story.
